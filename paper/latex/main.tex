\begin{abstract}

Urban street network redundancy provides alternatives and resilience, but requires additional infrastructure and cost. This study develops the concept of \enquote{treeness}---how closely a network resembles a minimal version of itself stripped of all redundancy---to formally measure how cities have evolved or been designed to balance this trade-off under spatial embedding constraints. It defines two indicators of treeness---link redundancy and length redundancy---then calculates them for every urban area in the world. Every urban street network is more tree-like than the baseline social and transport networks considered here. Street network treeness also has a small coefficient of variation around the world, despite cities' diverse histories, sizes, and geographies---suggesting that it converges to a characteristic scale that balances infrastructure cost versus structural redundancy, constrained by pragmatic design and engineering limitations imposed by quasiplanarity. We argue that treeness can enrich scientific theories of cities as well as planning processes for more connected, sustainable urbanization.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Redundancy in urban street networks comes with a trade-off. On one hand, it provides options, alternatives, and thus resilience to disruption \citep{barthelemy_morphogenesis_2017,boeing_2024_resilient}. On the other hand, redundancy requires additional infrastructure and thus higher capital and maintenance costs \citep{cardillo_2006_structural,tero_2010_rules}. This trade-off constrains how cities grow and thereby shapes the resulting urban form \citep{barthelemy_2022_spatial}. As policymakers increasingly emphasize connected, sustainable urbanization practices, measures of \enquote{treeness}---how closely a network resembles a counterfactual minimalist version of itself stripped of all redundancy---could identify vulnerabilities and reveal how cities have evolved or been designed to balance this optimization.

This study measures urban treeness to ask: to what extent are cities' street networks tree-like? It defines two indicators of treeness---link redundancy and length redundancy---which it calculates for every urban area's street network in the world. We find that \textit{every} urban street network in the world is more tree-like than the other baseline networks to which we compare them. Larger cities and less-hilly cities are less tree-like, which we argue aligns with theories of urbanization as a space-filling process. But overall, we find that urban treeness has a characteristic scale around the world, without outliers. This demonstrates that cities of diverse histories, sizes, and geographies have converged toward a typical balance of real-world trade-offs of network redundancy benefits and costs, constrained by spatial embedding and the requirements of functional transport.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. First it introduces the theory of spatial network treeness and reviews the mathematical formalism of trees in graph theory. Then it explains our methods for measuring treeness across the world's urban street networks, including data sources, analyses, baselines, and robustness checks. Next it presents our findings on urban treeness and its statistical and geographical properties. Finally it discusses the implications of these findings for theorizing urban form and for planning more connected, sustainable cities.

\section{Theory}

\subsection{A City Is Not a Tree}

In 1965, design theorist Christopher Alexander wrote a short paper provocatively titled \enquote{A City Is Not a Tree} \citep{alexander_city_1965}. In it, he argued that urban planners---working centrally and top-down---tend to envision and design cities as hierarchical tree-like structures, whereas successful traditional cities evolve naturally as semi-lattices. That is, over-planned cities' street networks, neighborhoods, and administrative districts organize into hierarchical branching structures rather than interconnected lattices \citep{huang_city_2022}. The former implies efficiency and functional segregation. The latter implies \enquote{messy} interconnectedness and complexity. Alexander's claim was at once both descriptive and normative: cities \textit{are} not trees, and planners \textit{should} not plan cities as trees.

This paper became an influential landmark across urban studies and presaged complexity science's subsequent applications to cities \citep{rauber_alexanders_2018,huang_city_2022}. Alexander's argument dovetailed with the findings of emerging spatial network science that linked urban accessibility and resilience to interconnectedness and complexity in urban form \citep{jiang_complex-network_2016,park_network_2015}. However, Alexander adopted the language of \enquote{trees} metaphorically, merely gesturing at the mathematical definition of trees, let alone measuring whether a city actually is one empirically \citep{harary_city_1976}. Yet mathematics does provide a precise formalization of trees and their properties that allows us to model and measure cities---particularly their networks' redundancy and vulnerability---in useful ways.

\subsection{Network Treeness}\label{sec:theory_network_treeness}

Graph theory is the branch of discrete mathematics that studies networks \citep{trudeau_introduction_1994}. A graph, $G = (N, E)$, consists of a set of nodes, $N$, and a set of edges, $E$, that link pairs of nodes \citep{barthelemy_spatial_2011}. For example, we could model a street network as a spatial graph with nodes representing intersections/dead-ends and edges representing street segments, with both the nodes and the edges embedded in space \citep{fischer_spatial_2014,porta_2006_network,barthelemy_modeling_2008}.

Formally, a tree is a type of graph that is connected and acyclic \citep{trudeau_introduction_1994}. If $G$ is connected, then for all $u, v \in N$ there exists a sequence of edges that starts at $u$ and ends at $v$. And if $G$ is acyclic, then it contains no cycles, meaning there are no paths that start and end at the same node without retracing any edge. In other words, a tree contains exactly one path between any two nodes and every edge is a single point of failure \citep{newman_networks:_2010}. Because the minimum number of edges needed to connect $n$ nodes is naturally $n - 1$, if a connected graph $G$ contains $n = |N|$ nodes and $m = |E|$ edges, then $G$ is a tree if and only if:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:tree_edge_count}
    m = n - 1
\end{equation}

From this we can derive a graph's circuit rank, $\rho$, which is the number of edges that must be removed to reduce it to a spanning tree:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:circuit_rank}
    \rho = m - n + c
\end{equation}

where $c$ is the number of components in the graph. By definition, a connected graph has $c = 1$ and a tree therefore has $\rho = 0$.

For our purposes, treeness entails two important properties for studying urban networks: redundancy and vulnerability \citep{xie_2007_measuring,barthelemy_morphogenesis_2017,barthelemy_2022_spatial}. Since a tree has only one path between any two nodes, if any edge is removed the network becomes disconnected. Accordingly trees have zero redundancy and are thus vulnerable to disruption (e.g., traffic congestion, collisions, floods, earthquakes, wildfires, terrorism) with no alternative routing options. In contrast, a less tree-like network offers multiple paths between nodes, providing redundancy and resilience to disruption when things go wrong \citep{barthelemy_spatial_2011}. Given these practical limitations of trees, one might expect that most cities evolved or were designed to incorporate at least some redundancy---which brings us to a graph-theoretic interpretation of Alexander's original metaphorical conjecture that cities are not trees.

One could empirically test this by measuring the treeness of a city's street network. For example, we could use the network's minimum spanning tree (MST) as a null model to compare against the full network. The MST is the tree that connects all of $G$'s nodes and minimizes some total edge weight---e.g., total length in the case of urban streets \citep{barthelemy_morphogenesis_2017}. Let $T$ be $G$'s MST.\@ We calculate $T$'s total length, $L_T$, as:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:total_edge_length}
    L_T = \sum_{\{u, v\} \in E_T} \ell_{u, v}
\end{equation}

where $\ell_{u, v}$ is the length of the edge linking nodes $u$ and $v$, and $E_T$ is the edge set of $T$. Although the MST does not provide \enquote{last-step} access to the block frontages, it represents the minimal necessary infrastructure to preserve reachability across the network \citep{cardillo_2006_structural,barthelemy_2022_spatial}. It thus offers a useful null model: the counterfactual network that connects all the nodes at the minimal cost, and therefore a theoretical lower bound for cost-constrained infrastructure networks. By comparing the full network modeled by $G$ to its MST $T$ (as in \autoref{fig:network_examples}) we could measure how tree-like $G$ is by how much redundancy it contains beyond $T$'s minimal version stripped of all redundancy.

\subsection{Are Some Cities Trees?}

Many geographical systems are trees, including natural systems like river networks and artificial systems like municipal stormwater drainage piping \citep{hesarkazzazi_assessing_2020,sanchirico_river_2025}. The latter suggests that certain physical urban infrastructure could be tree-like due to costs or efficiency. Given that, are some cities' street networks actually (or nearly) trees?

Transportation scholars have considered urban street networks as trees to distinguish branching structures from circuit-based networks. \citet{xie_2007_measuring} define road-network treeness as the complement of arterial circuitness, distinguishing tree-like arterial systems from ring, web, and beltway forms. \citet{levinson_2012_network} applies a related empirical measure to US metropolitan areas, defining treeness as the share of total street length that belongs to branches rather than circuits. \citet{han_2020_classification} classify urban street networks by tree-like features such as T-intersections, cul-de-sacs, penetrating streets, and cells. Together, this literature treats treeness as a measurable street network property through arterial structure, branch/circuit classification, or local design features.

This raises a broader theoretical question: what does treeness mean for a spatial infrastructure network? Street networks' spatial embedding makes them \textit{quasiplanar}: largely constrained to the Earth's surface, but punctuated by occasional nonplanar bridges, tunnels, overpasses, and underpasses \citep{boeing_planarity_2020}. Spatial network science treats trees as the minimum connected infrastructure against which redundancy, efficiency, and robustness can be evaluated \citep{barthelemy_morphogenesis_2017}. \citet{buhl_2006_topological} place self-organized street networks between tree structures and planar triangulations, finding that tree-like settlements are more vulnerable while meshed forms are more robust and efficient. \citet{cardillo_2006_structural} compare urban street samples to minimum spanning trees and greedy triangulations, interpreting real street networks as occupying a cost-efficiency space between minimally and highly connected planar forms. \citet{barthelemy_2022_spatial} formalizes this broader trade-off in spatial networks: links added beyond a minimum spanning tree raise infrastructure cost while improving accessibility, efficiency, and fault tolerance \citep[cf.][]{tero_2010_rules}.

These trade-offs matter for urban planning and geography because street network treeness shapes how cities function. \citet{huang_city_2022} revisit Alexander's conjecture empirically, relating meshedness and other street-network indicators to urban life across multiple cities. \citet{barringtonleigh_2019_global} measure street-network disconnectedness worldwide and classify networks across forms ranging from grids to dendritic (i.e., tree-like) and circuitous layouts. \citet{boeing_2024_resilient} shows that disruption vulnerability is lower in street networks with more connectivity, less dependence on chokepoints, and more direct routes.

The literature therefore establishes treeness as theoretically and practically important, and treeness indicators could thus quantify the vulnerability and (inverse) redundancy of urban street networks around the world. However, this literature remains limited by small samples, regional scope, narrow measures, typological proxies, or indirect indicators such as meshedness and disconnectedness. This leaves our central empirical question open: how tree-like are cities around the world, and what can this tell us about the balance between redundancy's benefits and costs?

\section{Methods}

This study addresses this and asks: to what extent are cities' street networks tree-like? We model the street networks of every urban area in the world, then find each network's MST to measure its link redundancy and length redundancy. Here we describe our data sources, calculations, and analyses, including baseline comparisons and robustness checks.

\subsection{Data Sources}

We use the models of the street networks of every urban area in the world from \citet{boeing_urban_2026}. These are 10,351 undirected graphs where nodes represent intersections/dead-ends and edges represent street segments \citep{boeing_topological_2025}. Each graph edge has a weight attribute representing its length in meters. We use undirected graphs because active transportation such as walking is undirected, as is the underlying infrastructure itself, even if certain vehicles face flow direction constraints on some streets.

\subsection{Measuring Treeness}

For each of these urban area street networks, let $G$ be the full network's undirected graph and let $T$ be its length-weighted MST (or minimum spanning forest if $G$ is not fully connected, as discussed below) as a null model. We compare $G$ to $T$ to measure its treeness.

One such topological indicator of this treeness could be the \textit{link redundancy}, akin to a normalized version of the circuit rank. This would measure how tree-like the network is in terms of how many structurally redundant links exist beyond those necessary to uniquely connect the nodes \citep{buhl_2006_topological}. However, this is purely topological and ignores the spatially-embedded network's edge geometries. A geometric equivalent could be the \textit{length redundancy}, which measures the share of the full network's total length that is in excess of the MST counterfactual. This would indicate how much extra infrastructure was built beyond the minimum necessary to provide reachability across the city.

First we define the link redundancy, $\theta$, as:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:link_redundancy_1}
    \theta = \frac{\rho}{m}
\end{equation}

where $\rho$ and $m$ are as defined in \autoref{eq:circuit_rank}. Note that \autoref{eq:link_redundancy_1} could be equivalently expressed as:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:link_redundancy_2}
    \theta = 1 - \frac{m_T}{m}
\end{equation}

where $m_T$ is the number of edges in $T$, which \autoref{eq:tree_edge_count} tells us is $n - 1$ if $G$ is connected (or $n - c$ otherwise). As redundancy and connectedness are conceptually related, we can test the relationship between $\theta$ and the average node degree, $\langle k \rangle$, a common measure of graph connectedness defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:avg_node_degree}
    \langle k \rangle = \frac{2m}{n}
\end{equation}

$\theta$ also shares some conceptual similarity with the normalized meshedness coefficient, $\alpha$, which appears in transport engineering \citep{levinson_2012_network,cardillo_2006_structural,buhl_2006_topological} as the ratio of a connected graph's circuit rank to the maximum possible circuit rank of a connected planar graph with the same node count:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:meshedness_coefficient}
    \alpha = \frac{\rho}{2 n - 5}
\end{equation}

This denominator represents an approximation because urban street networks are quasiplanar. It derives from Euler's formula which tells us that the maximum possible number of edges in a simple connected planar graph is:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:max_planar_edges}
    m_{\text{max}} = 3 n - 6
\end{equation}

Substituting this into \autoref{eq:circuit_rank} gives us the maximum possible circuit rank, i.e., the denominator of \autoref{eq:meshedness_coefficient}:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:max_planar_circuit_rank}
     \rho_{\text{max}} = (3 n - 6) - n + 1 = 2 n - 5
\end{equation}

However, by combining Equations~\ref{eq:circuit_rank},~\ref{eq:avg_node_degree}, and~\ref{eq:meshedness_coefficient} we can also see that, as $n \to \infty$:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:alpha_limit}
    \alpha \to \frac{\langle k \rangle}{4} - 0.5
\end{equation}

Thus, for large graphs like urban-scale street networks, $\alpha$ is approximately an affine transformation of $\langle k \rangle$ and therefore offers minimal statistical information beyond $\langle k \rangle$ itself. This makes the link redundancy, $\theta$, a more informative topological indicator of treeness (and thus inverse redundancy) for comparison across city-sized street networks.

Beyond pure topology, we also calculate a geometric indicator---the length redundancy, $\lambda$---as a spatial corollary to $\theta$. It represents the share of the full network's length in excess of the MST's length and is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:length_redundancy}
    \lambda = 1 - \frac{L_T}{L}
\end{equation}

where $L_T$ is as defined in \autoref{eq:total_edge_length} and $L$ is the equivalent total length of $G$. Note the mathematical correspondence between Equations~\ref{eq:link_redundancy_2} and~\ref{eq:length_redundancy}. Both $\theta$ and $\lambda$ are bounded by
$\left[0, 1\right)$,  % chktex 9
assuming $m > 0$ and $L > 0$ for any nontrivial real-world network. These are both indicators of treeness, where $\theta = \lambda = 0$ indicates a perfect tree and higher values indicate divergence away toward greater redundancy.

\subsection{Analysis}\label{sec:methods_analysis}

We calculate these treeness indicators for every urban area in the world then analyze their distributions to describe the nature of urban treeness. To better understand how treeness relates to other network properties relevant to urban planning, we also calculate Pearson correlation coefficients, $r$, to test $\theta$ and $\lambda$'s relationships with each other, and with other urban variables. The latter come from \citet{boeing_urban_2026}, where their relevance and calculation are explained in detail. But, to summarize them here, they include the aforementioned total network length $L$ (meters), hilliness (the standard deviation of node elevations, meters), the aforementioned average node degree $\langle k \rangle$ (dimensionless), the Gini coefficient of node betweenness centralities (a dimensionless measure of concentrated importance in certain nodes), average circuity (a dimensionless measure of the streets' average deviation from straight lines), orientation entropy (a dimensionless measure of the disorder in street bearings), built-up area (km\textsuperscript{2}), intersection density (per km\textsuperscript{2} of built-up area), and population density (thousands of persons per km\textsuperscript{2} of built-up area). We take the natural logarithms of $L$ and built-up area for a better linear fit with our treeness indicators.

\subsection{Baselines and Robustness Checks}

To establish an empirical baseline of treeness, we also calculate $\theta$ for several other kinds of undirected networks available from the ICON\endnote{Available online at \url{https://icon.colorado.edu/}} database, including the MSN social network \citep{leskovec_planetary-scale_2008}, the European airline network \citep{cardillo_emergence_2013}, the Paris subway network \citep{roth_long-time_2012}, and the Delhi bus route network \citep{chatterjee_statistical_2016}. To do so, we use the $m$ and $n$ values reported in each reference and assume $c=1$, which introduces a merely negligible error when the number of components is small relative to $m$, as is normal. These baselines are not experimental controls or matched counterfactuals as they differ in spatial embedding, planarity, and scale. We use them only as reference points to contextualize the magnitude and range of urban street network treeness relative to other familiar network types.

We also conduct two robustness checks by recalculating $\theta$ and $\lambda$ for (1) a topologically corrected version of $G$, and (2) only the largest component of $G$. For the first, we topologically correct $G$ using the \texttt{neatnet} package \citep{fleischmann_adaptive_2026} to simplify complex digitization, for example by collapsing multiple nodes representing single real-world intersections or multiple edges representing centerlines of a single divided road. This tests whether excess edges arising merely as artifacts of digitization bias our results by making networks appear more redundant than they actually are.

Regarding the second check, urban street networks are usually fully connected in the real world: you can reach any node from any other node. However, controlled access points, ferries, and the imposition of an artificial perimeter on the graph can produce disjoint components. If this happens, the MST of the full graph is technically a minimum spanning forest instead, and $\theta$ will be biased upward by the number of components, $c$, in the graph because, per \autoref{eq:link_redundancy_1}, as $c$ increases, $\theta$ increases even if the actual redundancy of the network has not changed. Therefore, we recalculate $\theta$ and $\lambda$ for each graph's largest component only, to test whether disconnected graphs bias our results.

\section{Findings}

\subsection{Empirical Baselines}\label{sec:findings_empirical_baselines}

We first report the $\theta$ values calculated for the other \enquote{kinds} of networks as an analytical baseline. The MSN social network---an aspatial network---has a $\theta$ of 0.862. The European airline network---with spatially-embedded nodes but nonplanar, nonfixed, relational edges---is 0.875. The Paris subway network---a nonplanar spatial network with both nodes and edges embedded and fixed in space---is 0.581, and the Delhi bus route network---a quasiplanar spatial network with both nodes and edges embedded and fixed in space---is 0.638. In other words, we can interpret these baselines as indicating that 58--88\% of these networks' edges would have to be removed to reduce them to their spanning trees. This empirical reference range contextualizes the following findings.

\subsection{Empirical Street Network Treeness}

\autoref{tab:treeness_descriptive} presents descriptive statistics for our two indicators of treeness. Across all the world's urban areas, the link redundancy, $\theta$, ranges empirically from 0.031--0.458 with a mean of 0.281, a median of 0.288, and a standard deviation of 0.068. This indicates that only 28\% of the edges in the average urban street network would have to be removed to reduce it to its spanning tree. In fact, the maximum observed $\theta$ of 0.458 means that there is no street network in the world that even approaches the baseline $\theta$ values of the other kinds of networks reported in \autoref{sec:findings_empirical_baselines}. That is, \textit{every} urban street network in the world is more tree-like than those baseline networks are.

\autoref{fig:histogram_indicators} illustrates the statistical distributions of $\theta$ and $\lambda$ across all the urban areas. It reveals a particularly interesting property of treeness at the urban-scale: both histograms are sharply peaked with narrow tails and zero extreme outliers (observations beyond $3\times$ the interquartile range). In fact, their coefficients of variation ($\sigma$ / $\mu$) are just 0.242 ($\theta$) and 0.205 ($\lambda$). These are bounded indicators, but most observations cluster fairly tightly around their mean. In other words, the treeness of urban street networks has a characteristic scale despite the scale-invariance of city size and the wide variety of urban forms and contexts.

In spite of this characteristic scale, there are some differences in different parts of the world (\autoref{tab:treeness_descriptive}). For example, Latin American and Caribbean cities have the highest mean $\theta$ and $\lambda$ values, while Oceania has the lowest. However, except for Oceania, all regions' means fall within one standard deviation of both indicators' worldwide means. Among megacities with at least 10 million residents, Buenos Aires is the least tree-like urban area ($\theta=0.415$, $\lambda=0.555$) and Bangkok is the most ($\theta=0.131$, $\lambda=0.290$). \autoref{fig:ba_vs_bangkok} shows typical neighborhoods in each to illustrate why: Buenos Aires has a lattice-like grid across the city, whereas Bangkok's network epitomizes a tree with its dendritic, branching dead-ends.

\autoref{fig:scatter_regressions} shows that both $\theta$ and $\lambda$ have statistically significant relationships with all the other urban variables. For example, both strongly and positively correlate with $\langle k \rangle$, indicating that more-connected networks are less tree-like, which is expected given the mathematical expressions of these measures. Both moderately and negatively correlate with the Gini coefficient of node betweenness centralities, indicating that more tree-like networks are more prone to importance being heavily concentrated in a few chokepoints nodes. Both moderately and negatively correlate with average circuity, indicating that more tree-like networks tend to be more geometrically circuitous/less efficient. They moderately and negatively correlate with orientation entropy, indicating that more tree-like networks are less consistently grid-like in their street bearings. Both $\theta$ (weakly) and $\lambda$ (moderately) positively correlate with both $L$ and built-up area, indicating that larger cities---in terms of total street length or urban area size---tend to be less tree-like. Both weakly correlate with intersection density (positively) and population density (negatively), indicating that more tree-like cities have sparser intersection densities but denser resident populations. Finally, both weakly but negatively correlate with the standard deviation of node elevations, indicating that hillier cities tend to be more tree-like.

\subsection{Robustness Checks}

We observe that our empirical findings are robust against re-analyzing with topologically corrected graphs. The global mean $\theta$ drops only slightly from 0.28 to 0.26 and mean $\lambda$ drops from 0.44 to 0.41, as expected since topological correction should remove at least some edges. The relationships in \autoref{fig:scatter_regressions} remain stable, with the same signs and similar magnitudes. We observe even greater robustness of the findings when re-analyzing only the largest component (in fact, in the median urban area, 99.8\% of the edges belong to the largest component). The global mean $\theta$ remains 0.28 and mean $\lambda$ remains 0.44. Again the relationships in \autoref{fig:scatter_regressions} remain stable, with the same signs and similar magnitudes. Overall, these checks show that our findings are effectively unbiased by digitization artifacts or disconnected graphs.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A City Is Sometimes Kind of a Tree}

A tree is a network with zero redundancy and thus no resilience to disruption. This study measured urban treeness to ask how tree-like cities' street networks are. We find that all cities contain at least some redundancy, but many contain surprisingly little. In that sense, Alexander's conjecture was literally correct: no city in the world is a perfect tree. However, many are very tree-like with $\theta$ values as low as 0.03---indicating what we might call a \textit{quasitree}. Redundancy is negligible in such cases as merely 3\% of links need to be removed to eliminate all alternative network paths. Thus, in Alexander's parlance, we observe that a city is sometimes kind of a tree.

Tree-like cities are not outliers or anomalies. Rather, we find that every urban street network in the world is more tree-like than the baseline networks we compared them to. The world's least tree-like (most redundant) city has merely 45\% of its edges in excess of its MST---much lower than the reference range. And in the average city, only 28\% of edges would need to be removed to eliminate all redundancy. We also observe a close relationship between topological and geometric treeness: $\theta$ and $\lambda$ strongly correlate, but $\lambda$ tends to be higher since we are working with length-weighted MSTs.

Treeness also has significant relationships with other urban indicators of practical interest. More tree-like networks are less well-connected, more circuitous, and have more concentrated chokepoints. Larger cities tend to be less tree-like, consistent with the notion that as cities grow, their two-dimensional space-filling process introduces redundancies into the network. This process may also be shaped by topography, since we observe that hillier cities are more tree-like: rough terrain can constrain the space-filling process of urban growth toward a dendritic form along foothills and canyons rather than forming a complete homogeneous lattice.

\subsection{Urban Treeness as Constrained Redundancy}

Although larger cities tend to be less tree-like, $\theta$ and $\lambda$ only weakly and moderately correlate with city size, respectively. City size is scale-free, spanning many orders of magnitude from small towns to megacity outliers like Bangkok and Buenos Aires. Urban treeness, however, varies within a much narrower range: both indicators cluster relatively tightly around their means with no outliers, and most regional averages fall close to the worldwide average. This suggests that urban street networks converge toward a characteristic scale of redundancy despite vast differences in city size, planning history, topography, and sociopolitical context.

Given the practical costs and benefits of redundant infrastructure, this range could reflect a basin of attraction that represents a reasonable optimization of trade-offs. Cities need some redundancy to support circulation, access, and resilience, but each additional street segment consumes land, entails capital and maintenance costs, and requires interfacing with the surrounding built environment. Rather than maximizing redundancy, cities might settle within a reasonable range that balances the trade-offs of redundancy's costs and benefits.

Beyond optimization, quasiplanarity can help further explain this constrained range. For example, we can substitute Equations~\ref{eq:max_planar_edges} and~\ref{eq:max_planar_circuit_rank} into the denominator and numerator, respectively, of \autoref{eq:link_redundancy_1} to find the maximum possible $\theta$ of a simple connected planar graph:

\begin{equation}
    \theta_{\text{max}} = \frac{2n - 5}{3n - 6}
\end{equation}

which approaches $2 / 3$ as $n \to \infty$. Though expensive, quasiplanar cities could even exceed this limit by building more nonplanar links like bridges, tunnels, overpasses, and underpasses.

Yet \textit{all} of the world's street networks fall \textit{well} below this theoretical upper limit. Why? If we combine Equations~\ref{eq:avg_node_degree} and~\ref{eq:max_planar_edges}, we can see that a maximally connected planar graph has $\langle k \rangle \to 6$ as $n \to \infty$. Although real-world street networks certainly \textit{could} be built with many nonplanar links---or even as block triangulations---they rarely \textit{are} because 5- and 6-way intersections complicate traffic control, navigation, and urban design. A more practical upper bound for most cities might instead be an idealized perfect grid, where $\langle k \rangle \to 4$ and therefore $m_{\text{grid}} = 2n$, per \autoref{eq:avg_node_degree}. Thus, if we substitute $m_{\text{grid}}$ into \autoref{eq:link_redundancy_2}, we can find the asymptotic $\theta$ of a perfect grid:

\begin{equation}
    \theta_{\text{grid}} = 1 - \frac{n - 1}{2n} = \frac{n + 1}{2n}
\end{equation}

which approaches $1/2$ as $n \to \infty$. This upper limit is indeed close to our maximum observed $\theta$ of 0.458 in this worldwide study.

However, quasiplanarity is purely topological: it does not constrain geometry and therefore cannot directly explain the observed range of $\lambda$. That is, planners \textit{could} build a street network of truly exotic geometry and extreme circuity. Why don't they? Typical urban treeness may represent an optimization of both link and length redundancy---but one that is also constrained by the urban design practicalities imposed by spatial embedding and real-world function. Although quasiplanarity sets an upper limit topologically, other pragmatic concerns like intersection geometries, block shapes, traffic control, rights-of-way, underlying terrain, construction and maintenance costs, and the limited utility of adding every possible link collectively constrain the world's realized street network forms.

\subsection{Implications and Future Research}

Prior studies have explored treeness through small samples, theory, metaphor, or typologies, and have measured related properties such as meshedness, circuitness, dendricity, disconnectedness, and resilience. But here we directly measure treeness against a minimal cost/infrastructure null model for every city in the world. It reveals that street network treeness has a characteristic scale---despite cities' vast differences in size, history, terrain, and culture---suggesting that local context does not fully subsume universal processes of urban growth. Cities are neither simply trees nor non-trees. They evolve to something between minimal connected structures and fully redundant lattices as spatial embedding, quasiplanarity, real-world functional requirements, and infrastructure costs constrain how much redundancy they accumulate.

For practitioners, these indicators help identify high-treeness areas to flag neighborhoods with few alternatives for evacuation, emergency access, or detours during closures---which can inform emergency access reviews, evacuation planning, and disaster vulnerability assessments. They can also help prioritize new connector projects as planners evaluate whether proposed streets actually reduce network brittleness. This matters because infrastructure is expensive to build and maintain. Rather than producing dendritic layouts or merely maximizing redundancy everywhere, planners can strategically target new connections where vulnerability and travel demand justify them, while avoiding overbuilding where costs exceed benefits.

This study's limitations create opportunities for future research. First, the MST serves as a null model rather than a feasible street plan because it preserves node reachability rather than frontage access, capacity, hierarchy, travel demand, or route quality. Future work should explore alternative null models as analytical counterfactuals. Second, our indicators measure treeness (and inverse redundancy), but do not directly measure flow-based properties like congestion, travel time, mode choice, safety, access, or equity. Future work should test how treeness shapes these dynamics. Third, the few baseline networks here serve to contextualize magnitude but should be expanded as future counterfactuals. Finally, we examined street networks at the urban scale: future research should investigate treeness at finer scales to explore within-city heterogeneity and how network structure changes over time through urban evolution and growth.

\section{Conclusion}

Physical network redundancy comes with a trade-off: alternatives and resilience versus additional infrastructure and costs. This study asked how tree-like cities' street networks are by measuring their treeness to assess how much infrastructure exists beyond a counterfactual minimum stripped of all redundancy. Cities are not exactly trees---but many are surprisingly close, and urban treeness demonstrates a characteristic scale despite cities' vast differences and outliers in size, history, terrain, and culture. This suggests that street networks converge toward an optimal range that balances the benefits of circulation and resilience against infrastructure costs and real-world practical constraints imposed by their spatial embedding and functional requirements. Network treeness provides a lens to understand and explain urban form and helps practitioners identify fragility for targeted interventions as we pursue more connected, sustainable urban futures.
